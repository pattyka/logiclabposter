1. Context & Rationale

When the machine thinks, the child’s mind does less of it

A ten-year-old on a tram told his father that ChatGPT had finished his homework, and the father was pleased. In the years the reasoning mind is built, children are learning to skip the act of thinking. The binding constraint is not pedagogy but adherence.

Cognitive Debt

MIT Media Lab, 2025: minutes after writing an essay with an AI assistant, 83% could not quote one sentence of it, against 11% unaided, and the AI group showed the weakest EEG connectivity of three conditions. Adult sample, preprint, and the mechanism a child meets every afternoon.

FIG 1. Significant alpha-band brain connections, Brain-only vs ChatGPT: 79 vs 42 (EEG dDTF, N=54, p<.05).
The Critical Window

Executive function, the inhibition, working memory and reasoning beneath later analytical thought, develops fastest between ages 7 and 11. What is exercised now consolidates; what is left idle is harder to build later. And the device arrives mid-window: half of children own a smartphone by 11.

FIG 2. Complex executive-function trajectory by age (ages 5–17) against the rise of smartphone ownership.
The Collision

Children aged 8–12 spend 5 h 33 min a day on entertainment screens, teens 8 h 39 min, up 17% in two years. The scarce resource in a child’s education is no longer content. It is attention.

FIG 3. Daily entertainment screen media by age group, 2019 vs 2021 (+17%).
What I set out to build and test

Pedagogical. Make reasoning learnable the way arithmetic is: by practice, not exposition. The child builds an argument, finds the broken step, weighs evidence.

Technical. A native app that trades completed practice for parent-configured screen time, enforced by the OS, not by the app’s goodwill, plus an AI tutor that may only ask.

Research. Test against a control whether reasoning measurably improves, and whether the reward erodes the motivation it is meant to build; publish whatever the result.

The hypothesis: make thinking the price of screen time

Premack (1959): a higher-probability behaviour can reinforce a lower-probability one. Screen entertainment is a child’s highest-probability behaviour and reasoning practice among the lowest, so finishing a lesson earns minutes that unlock the apps the parent chose, and iOS, not the app, holds the lock. The honest objection, the overjustification effect, is treated as a design constraint and measured, not assumed away.
